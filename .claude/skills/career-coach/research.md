# Career Coach Skill — Research Basis

This document records the evidence base for every framework in the career-coach skill, what was evaluated and rejected, and why each choice was made. It exists so that future revisions can be grounded in evidence, not accumulated assumption.

**Research conducted:** July 3, 2026  
**Scope:** Executive career coaching (CTO/VPE/Sr Dir level)

---

## Frameworks Currently in the Skill

### Schein's Career Anchors

**Source:** Edgar H. Schein, MIT Sloan. Developed from a longitudinal study of MIT Sloan MBA alumni beginning in the 1960s. First published as *Career Dynamics* (Addison-Wesley, 1978); instrument (*Career Anchors: Discovering Your Real Values*, Pfeiffer, 1985; 4th ed. with John Van Maanen, Wiley, 2013).

**Evidence quality: Moderate.** Genuine peer-reviewed literature exists — more than any other framework in this skill.

- Barclay, Chapman & Brown (2013), *Journal of Career Development*: Factor analyses on the COI do not reliably reproduce Schein's 8-factor structure — items load differently depending on sample.
- Costigan, Gurbuz & Sigri (2018), *Journal of Career Development*: 9-factor structure fits better than the original 8.
- Worcester empirical investigation (eprints.worc.ac.uk): COI's weighting process reduces validity; no published norms; raw scores alone are misleading.
- *Construct validity review (ResearchGate)*: "There is not definitive evidence on how many independent career anchor types exist and how independent these eight types are."

**Practitioner standing:** High. Widely used by ICF-credentialed coaches and executive search firms for ~50 years. Called "the industry standard" for values-based career discovery.

**Known limitations:**
- Original sample was MIT Sloan men in the 1960s — narrow and elite.
- Self-reported values, not independently assessed capabilities.
- Static framing; anchors can shift over a career.
- The instrument's measurement properties are weaker than its practitioner reputation suggests.

**Why kept:** The 8 anchors remain useful as reflective categories for surfacing values even if the COI instrument has measurement weaknesses. The Managerial Competence vs. Technical/Functional Competence distinction is particularly relevant at CTO/VPE level where this tension is common and real.

**How to use correctly:** As a reflective prompt and conversation structure, not as a psychometric score. Don't administer the COI literally and score it — use the anchor categories as a vocabulary for probing.

### Opportunity Fit Assessment (formerly "Five Fit Dimensions")

**Original claim:** "Five Fit Dimensions" was presented as a named framework. It is not. No such named framework exists in published career coaching, organizational psychology, or practitioner literature.

**Actual source:** A composite drawing on legitimate but separately-sourced constructs:

- *Person-job fit* and *person-organization fit*: Amy Kristof-Brown, multiple papers in *Personnel Psychology* and *Journal of Applied Psychology*, including a 2005 meta-analysis of 179 studies. Covers: person-job fit, person-organization fit, person-group fit, and person-vocation fit. Each predicts different outcomes (job satisfaction, organizational commitment, intent to leave).
- *Growth Trajectory*: Corresponds to Newport's career capital concept and the organizational literature on developmental stretch assignments.
- *Problem Fit*: Closest academic cousin is "calling" research (Wrzesniewski, 1997, *Journal of Organizational Behavior*) — tenure predicts seeing work as a calling, not pre-existing passion.

**Why retained (as a heuristic, not a named framework):** The five dimensions are sensible and usable; the problem was the false claim of being a named framework. The skill now labels this accurately as a synthesized evaluation heuristic grounded in P-E fit research.

**Key source:** Kristof-Brown, A.L. et al. (2005). Consequences of individuals' fit at work: A meta-analysis of person-job, person-organization, person-group, and person-vocation fit. *Personnel Psychology*, 58(2), 281–342.

### Career Capital (Newport)

**Source:** Cal Newport, *So Good They Can't Ignore You: Why Skills Trump Passion in the Quest for Work You Love* (Business Plus/Grand Central Publishing, 2012). Newport is a computer science professor at Georgetown; this is a practitioner/pop-business book, not a research publication.

**Evidence quality: Low-moderate.** Newport cites legitimate research but the book itself is primarily anecdotal:

- Amy Wrzesniewski (Yale): Study of college administrative assistants found tenure predicts seeing work as a calling — passion follows mastery, not the reverse. (*Journal of Organizational Behavior*, 1997.) Newport cites this correctly.
- Anders Ericsson's deliberate practice research (*Psychological Review* and others) underpins the "build rare skills" argument — though Macnamara et al. (2014, *Psychological Science*) meta-analysis found practice explains less variance than Ericsson claimed.

**Known limitations:**
- Book is heavily anecdotal; conclusions outrun the evidence.
- Meritocracy assumption: assumes rare and valuable skills get rewarded equitably. Research on gender/racial pay gaps shows this is not true.
- Survivorship bias: Newport studied people who succeeded by building skills; didn't study those who built excellent skills but still had poor careers.
- Gives limited guidance on *which* type of capital to build for an executive transition specifically.

**Why kept:** The underlying insight — that great work tends to precede passion rather than the reverse — is supported by Wrzesniewski's research. Useful as one evaluative dimension (does this role build rare and valuable capabilities?).

**How to use correctly:** One lens, not a complete framework. The question "what will you be better at in two years?" is worth asking about every opportunity.

### Regret Minimization (Bezos)

**Source:** Jeff Bezos, 1997 Academy of Achievement interview (and repeated in subsequent interviews). Not a published framework; no named inventor besides Bezos as autobiographer. Not cited in any academic career coaching literature.

**Evidence quality: Anecdote + adjacent research.** Adjacent support:
- Anticipated regret research (Zeelenberg & Pieters, 2007, *Journal of Consumer Psychology*): anticipated regret influences decisions; "inaction regret" is more persistent than "action regret" long-term (Gilovich & Medvec, 1994, *Psychological Review*).
- Temporal self-appraisal: projecting to a far-future vantage point reduces present-biased thinking (Hershfield, 2011, *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience*).
- The specific "age 80" projection is Bezos's invention, not established by research.

**Known limitations:**
- Selection bias: worked for Bezos, who had financial capital, no dependents, and a supportive spouse. Does not generalize to all risk profiles.
- Inaction regret inflation: people underestimate how much they adapt to negative outcomes (Gilbert, 2006, *Stumbling on Happiness*). May systematically over-push toward bold action.
- Not useful for comparing multiple competing alternatives — it is a go/no-go reframing device.

**Why kept (narrowed):** Useful when the user is frozen by risk aversion about a single choice. Not useful for comparing two or three real competing offers. The skill now scopes it correctly.

### Ikigai

**Source — what it actually is:** Two separate things conflated under one name.

*Traditional ikigai*: A Japanese philosophical concept meaning "reason for being" (iki = life; gai = value/worth). Not a career framework in Japanese culture — it is a life orientation toward finding joy in everyday activities. Japanese research on ikigai's relationship to longevity exists (Murakami; Buettner's Blue Zones research), but this is about life purpose broadly, not career planning.

*The 4-circle Venn diagram used in coaching*: A Western invention. Created by Spanish blogger Andrés Zuzunaga around 2011 (he called it "Propósito"). British entrepreneur Marc Winn merged Zuzunaga's diagram with Buettner's Blue Zones/ikigai concept in a 2014 blog post. The false attribution to Japanese wisdom was created then. Documented in Ken Mogi's *The Little Book of Ikigai* (2017, Quercus) and in Marc Winn's own public admission.

**Evidence quality: Minimal.** No peer-reviewed validation for the 4-circle diagram. A 2023 paper in MDPI's *Societies* attempts to apply it but cannot cite empirical validation for the diagram itself.

**Known limitations:**
- The attribution to Japanese wisdom tradition is false.
- "What you can be paid for" selects for existing labor market demand, which may lag or undervalue emerging roles.
- At executive level, the four circles rarely all align; insistence on intersection can produce paralysis or guilt.
- Produces "vision board" outputs rather than actionable next steps.

**Why kept (as heuristic, with corrected attribution):** Its visual clarity is useful as a conversation starter about values intersection. Labeled accurately as a Western-invented heuristic, not a Japanese framework, and not as a filter that must be passed.

## Frameworks Added

### Ibarra's Working Identity

**Source:** Herminia Ibarra, *Working Identity: Unconventional Strategies for Reinventing Your Career* (Harvard Business School Press, 2003). Ibarra is a professor at INSEAD and London Business School. The book is based on a 3-year longitudinal study of 39 executives undergoing major career transitions.

**Evidence quality: Moderate-High for executive transitions specifically.** Longitudinal case study methodology (more rigorous than Newport's anecdotal approach). The core finding has been cited and built upon in organizational psychology:

- Career change does not happen through introspection first, then action. It happens through doing: experimenting with new professional activities, building new networks, and iterating on identity.
- People who spend too long in values-clarification and "find yourself" exercises before acting typically stall. Action creates the evidence that revises identity.

**Why added:** The most directly applicable research-backed framework for executive career transitions. Directly addresses the trap of over-analyzing options and under-experimenting. Relevant to the user's current search: if they are heavily weighing introspective exercises (what are my values? where do I want to be in 5 years?) before taking exploratory actions, Ibarra's research suggests the causation should run the other way.

**How to use:** When the user is stuck in analysis, blocked on a choice, or spending more energy evaluating than experimenting — surface the Ibarra frame. The right question is "what experiments can you run to gather real data?" not "what do your values tell you to do?"

### Self-Determination Theory (SDT) — Needs Check

**Source:** Edward Deci & Richard Ryan, *Intrinsic Motivation and Self-Determination in Human Behavior* (Plenum, 1985). Extensively peer-reviewed for 40+ years. Applied to coaching: Spence & Oades (2011), "Coaching with self-determination in mind," *International Coaching Psychology Review* — the canonical paper connecting SDT to coaching practice. Multiple RCTs show SDT-aligned interventions improve well-being, goal attainment, and work engagement.

**Evidence quality: High — strongest empirical foundation of any framework in this skill.** Unlike Ikigai (no evidence), Regret Minimization (anecdote), or Career Capital (pop-business), SDT has decades of replicated peer-reviewed research across cultures and contexts.

**The three basic psychological needs:**
- **Autonomy** — agency over decisions, acting in accordance with one's values, not feeling controlled
- **Competence** — doing what you're best at, tackling challenges at the right level of difficulty
- **Relatedness** — genuine connection with the people you work with and for

Deprivation of any of these three needs predicts dissatisfaction and disengagement even when compensation is high. Satisfaction of all three is the strongest single predictor of sustainable motivation.

**Why added:** Gives a simple, evidence-backed diagnostic that can be applied to any opportunity: does this role satisfy the user's needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness? Particularly useful when an opportunity scores well financially but the user has a nagging sense something is wrong.

## Frameworks Considered and Rejected

### Holland's RIASEC (John Holland, 1959–1997)

**What it is:** Six personality/interest types (Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, Conventional). Most empirically validated career interest framework in existence. 50+ years of meta-analyses support predictive validity for career satisfaction and occupational choice (Nauta, 2010, *Journal of Vocational Behavior*).

**Why not added:** RIASEC was designed for occupational entry, not senior career transitions. At CTO/VPE level the relevant question is not "what kind of work interests me" (the user has answered that over 25 years) but "which specific senior role and organization fits." RIASEC doesn't differentiate well at this level.

### Savickas Career Construction Theory / Life Design

**What it is:** The dominant paradigm in academic career counseling since 2010s. Emphasizes career adaptability (Concern, Control, Curiosity, Confidence) and narrative identity (Savickas, *Career Counseling*, APA, 2011). PMC 2024 review confirms it is the most active area of career research internationally.

**Why not added:** The narrative/meaning-making approach is time-intensive and harder to operationalize in a structured coaching session. Designed for longer-term career counseling engagements, not decision support in an active job search. May be worth adding if the user's search extends and the work shifts from opportunity evaluation to deeper career identity work.

### MBTI

**Why rejected:** The broad scientific consensus in organizational psychology is that MBTI should not be used for career prescription. Erford (2025, *Journal of Counseling & Development*) reviewed 193 studies from 1999–2024 and found ongoing concerns about test-retest reliability. Pittenger (2005, *Psychometric Theory*) is the definitive published critique. The ICF does not recommend it as a primary framework.

### CliftonStrengths (Gallup StrengthsFinder)

**Why not added:** Widely used by coaches and has a large community. However, the technical report (Lopez et al., 2005) is commercially produced by Gallup and has not been rigorously externally validated. Independent replication is thin. ERIC review found insufficient evidence for higher education applications. Treat as a conversation-starting heuristic if the user brings it up — don't rely on it as a framework.
